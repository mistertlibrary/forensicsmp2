Start Here

Begin with the question every report must answer.

  1. Find your case in the A to Z list. Note the evidence on record and how deep the record runs.
  2. Open the discipline pages for the evidence in your case. Learn what each technique can and cannot show.
  3. Read the sources under Start Here, then search the library databases with the terms provided.
  4. Choose one technique to highlight. Ask what it showed, who tested it, and whether the result impacted the case.
  5. Cite at least three sources in APA as you go.

Big Questions

Questions that reach beyond a single case.

What forensic techniques, if any, helped investigators identify or rule out a suspect? How did the results move the case forward?

Start with the evidence, then follow it to the suspect.

  • fbi.govFBI Handbook of Forensic Services

    The FBI Laboratory's guide to collecting and submitting evidence.

  • nist.govScientific Foundation Reviews (NIST)

    NIST reviews of the data behind forensic methods.

How do investigators determine whether separate crimes are connected?

Investigators compare victims, methods, places, and physical evidence. Databases help, when agencies use them.

  • fbi.govSerial Murder: Multi-Disciplinary Perspectives for Investigators (FBI, 2008)

    Findings from an FBI symposium of experts on defining and investigating serial murder.

  • fbi.govSerial Murder: Pathways for Investigations (FBI, 2014)

  • murderdata.orgMurder Accountability Project

    A nonprofit that tracks unsolved homicides and flags clusters that may be connected.

  • maamodt.asp.radford.eduRadford/FGCU Serial Killer Database

    A university research database of serial killers, with case data.

How has forensic evidence changed cold-case investigations?

DNA databases and genetic genealogy have reopened decades-old cold cases and raised questions about privacy.

How reliable is a forensic method, and who decides?

Scientists, courts, and government panels do not always agree. Compare what each says about the same method.

  • doi.orgStrengthening Forensic Science in the United States (National Academies, 2009)

    The National Research Council reviews the scientific basis of forensic disciplines.

  • obamawhitehouse.archives.govForensic Science in Criminal Courts (PCAST, 2016)

    Presidential science advisers assess the validity of methods that compare features such as prints and marks.

  • justice.govDOJ Statement on the PCAST Report (2021)

    The Justice Department's response disputing parts of the PCAST report.

  • nist.govScientific Foundation Reviews (NIST)

    NIST finds that bitemark analysis lacks a sufficient scientific foundation.

  • ojp.govMorgan, Wrongful Convictions and Claims of False or Misleading Forensic Evidence (Journal of Forensic Sciences, 2023)

    A study of the forensic errors found in wrongful convictions.

  • exonerationregistry.orgNational Registry of Exonerations

    A university project documenting every known exoneration in the United States since 1989.

What distinguishes an expert-consensus report from an empirical study?

An expert-consensus report records what specialists agree on. An empirical study measures something and reports the data. See the paired readings.

Compare how a case was covered with what the record shows.

    The Supreme Court decision on pretrial publicity in the Sam Sheppard case.
